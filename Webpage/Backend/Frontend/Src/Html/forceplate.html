The instrument

What a force plate measures

A force plate measures the forces between a person’s feet and the ground during standing, walking, running or jumping.

Most plates only measure how hard you push straight down, and even these cost thousands of dollars. Measuring the knee adduction moment (KAM), a key warning sign for knee osteoarthritis, also needs sideways forces.

$30,000–50,000 For a multi-axis plate. A full gait lab for KAM costs over $150,000.

Why it matters

Who needs this measurement

I wanted to understand why injuries like mine kept coming back. I learned that how forces act on a joint can help explain repeated injuries and ways to prevent them. One widely studied measure is the knee adduction moment (KAM), which indicates how much load falls on the inner side of the knee during walking.

365 million people worldwide live with knee osteoarthritis

Most of them are older adults, and KAM is one of the best-studied predictors of whose knee will get worse. Measuring KAM requires a force plate that measures sideways forces. This usually means using a gait lab costing over $150,000, which few clinics can afford.

Professor Yoon guided the comparison. I prepared the CAD models, specified the materials, loads and supports, and determined which settings to keep consistent across all three designs.

Simulation workflow

Working through the simulation results

A simulation took several hours at times. Once it finished, I reviewed the output before deciding what to change for the next run.

I checked how the settings I had chosen affected the predicted behavior. Before trusting a run, I checked that the support forces added up to the load I applied (500 N sideways, 866 N down).

Simulation findings

What I learned from the simulations

A simple crosstalk model (Eq. 1) put the vertical force error at a 22° load near 0.2% for the hybrid and 2% for a bolted design. In ANSYS with a 30° load, corner friction took only 2.75 to 3.58% of the sideways force.

The simulation results placed my hybrid design between the research-grade and low-cost alternatives. I wrote a research proposal (SARC 2026) based on it and began making a prototype to see how the design would behave outside the simulation.

I still needed measurements from actual hardware to assess accuracy.

Mechanical design

From concept to buildable design

The simulation model became a design that could be built: a 600 × 400 mm aluminum top and base plate, four corner load cells on hardened rounded studs, and a 3-axis sensor in the center on a steel riser.

Each corner can be raised a little at a time, so all four press on the plate with about 50 N before a test.

Building it raised problems the simulation never showed. The corner load cells had M16 holes instead of M12, so a nut seat was made for the studs. The amplifiers turned out to be very sensitive: breadboards and loose jumper wires added resistance and picked up noise. Redoing the wiring and moving most connections to proper connectors brought the noise down to a few hundredths of a newton.

Readout code

Readout code written in Python

The plate's readout code is written in Python (MicroPython) and runs on the ESP32. It reads all eight 24-bit amplifiers at the same moment, removes spikes, and shows live forces, center of pressure and knee moment on a phone over Wi-Fi. Each test is saved as a CSV row.

The amplifier boards and the microcontroller are off-the-shelf parts. The wiring, the code that reads them and the phone readout were built for this project.

Test data

Standing on one leg

A 76.0 kg person stood on the left leg for 10 seconds, twice. The plate read 76.7 kg and 76.2 kg, and the center of pressure moved about 3 mm between the two trials.

Next steps toward measuring KAM

Measure the knee center position above the plate, record single-leg stance with the knee-moment readout, then compare barefoot, shoes and insoles. After that, calibrate sideways force with the pulley rig and move to faster electronics for walking.

Lessons and next steps

What the hardware taught me

What the simulation did not show

The simulation assumed perfect joints, and the real plate did not have them. Tiny gaps where parts were bolted together moved under load: after a 15 kg child stepped off, the center sensor still held 5 to 8 N of sideways force. Moving the plate changed the preload on one corner by almost 90 N, and the amplifiers and load cells needed time to warm up before the readings settled.

Next experiments

The pulley rig, weighted sled and reference load cell are ready. Next I will apply known sideways forces to see how much the corners really take, and measure the knee adduction moment in single-leg stance.

Limits of this readout

The readout runs at 10 Hz, enough for standing tests. Walking is usually recorded at about 1,000 Hz, and noise grows as the readout gets faster, so the next version needs a faster low-noise amplifier on a properly designed circuit board.

Conclusion

Prototype parts cost about $2,700

In parts for a plate that already reads vertical force and center of pressure, and is built to read sideways force and the static knee moment once calibration is done.

This prototype (parts) $2,700
Research 3-axis plate $30,000 to $50,000
Gait lab needed for KAM over $150,000

It is not a clinical tool yet. It reads at 10 Hz, has only been tested standing still, and the sideways-force channel is not calibrated yet. But it showed that one multi-axis sensor and four low-cost load cells can be built into a plate that reads body weight within about 1% before calibration, for less than a tenth of the price of a single research plate.

Whether this layout can measure everything KAM needs is the question I started with, and the next tests will answer it.
